# TX-31: Justin Early — Financial Disclosure Summary (2026 Cycle)

Justin Early is running for Texas's 31st Congressional District, a Williamson County-anchored seat (Round Rock, Georgetown, Killeen, Fort Cavazos) held since 2003 by Republican incumbent John Carter. One FEC committee reports 2026-cycle activity for Early: his principal campaign committee, **Justin Early for Congress** (C00907329). The committee reported **$51,641.00** in itemized outside contributions ($44,641.00 individual, $7,000.00 PAC), plus **$9,842.10** in in-kind contributions and **$32,800.00** in loans from the candidate himself — a combined funding base of roughly **$94,283**. Against that, the committee reported **$104,402.20** in itemized disbursements. This is a modest, early-stage challenger operation: no House Ethics Committee financial disclosure filing exists for Early in the data reviewed (see Takeaways), and several funding and spending patterns below point to a campaign still building basic infrastructure rather than running paid voter contact at scale.

## Major Spending

Itemized disbursements break down heavily toward back-office and vendor overhead rather than voter-facing advertising. **Administrative/Salary/Overhead Expenses** is the largest category by far at $66,122.72 (63.3% of spending, 113 transactions), followed by **Campaign Materials** at $13,110.20, an **Uncategorized** bucket at $9,842.10 (explained below), **Advertising Expenses** at $6,116.18, **Political Contributions** at $5,500.00, **Campaign Event Expenses** at $3,000.00, **Solicitation and Fundraising Expenses** at $898.36, and **Travel Expenses** at $262.64.

The single largest vendor is **1926 Prints** (Waco, TX) at $17,661.58 across several payments described as "campaign website and general consulting" — including a $10,000.00 payment on July 2, 2025 and a $5,000.00 payment on July 30, 2025, both in the campaign's first two months. **American Printing and Mailing** (Austin, TX) follows at $13,110.20 for physical printing. **Kenneth Flippin** (Taylor, TX) received $10,010.00 in consulting fees. Two out-of-state consulting firms round out the top vendors: **Sunrise Political Solutions** (La Mesa, CA), $8,709.44 for compliance services, and **ACCG** (Houston, TX), $7,762.64 in fundraising-consultant payments spread across three checks. **Johnson Smith Consulting LLC** (Lexington, KY) received $6,000.00 in direct consulting-fee payments plus an additional $2,500.00 routed through an in-kind reimbursement (see below), for $8,500.00 total. **Integrated Solutions: Political** (San Diego, CA) took $5,425.00, and **Polaris Campaigns Inc** (Chicago, IL) received $5,000.00 for digital advertising.

The $9,842.10 "Uncategorized" line, all attributed to payee "Early, Justin," is not a payment to the candidate personally — it's the FEC's standard double-entry treatment of in-kind contributions. Ten times this cycle, Early paid for a campaign expense (event venue rental, a fundraising consultant invoice, web services, printing, speech coaching, software) out of his own pocket and donated the value to the campaign; FEC rules require that value to be booked both as a receipt (Schedule A, "Contributions From the Candidate") and as a matching operating expenditure (Schedule B), so it appears twice in the raw data by design. The card/bulk-payment breakdown finds 5 lump payments here, 95.9% covered by memo-level itemized detail, and that detail confirms the actual vendors: the largest, a $6,249.43 "event venue rental," was paid to **The Vinyard at Florence**, a Texas event venue; a $2,500.00 "fundraising consultant invoice" went to Johnson Smith Consulting LLC (the same firm noted above); and smaller amounts went to Campaign Deputy, DKR Strategies LLC, SlickText, Peerspace (a venue/workspace booking platform), The Cove Theater, Phone Burner, and Google.

Two performance-fee payments to **Trey Taylor** (Broomfield, CO) in October 2025 are worth a note on how to read this data correctly: the raw transaction list shows two separate $3,000.00 "PERFORMANCE FEE" charges on different dates, which would suggest $6,000.00 spent on an event performer. A same-day $3,000.00 correction (negative-amount) row tied to one of those charges means the real, net cost to the campaign was $3,000.00, not $6,000.00 — which is why Taylor doesn't appear in a top-payees list despite showing up twice among the largest individual line items.

Finally, the campaign made two outbound political contributions this cycle: $3,000.00 to the **Texas Democratic Party** (split between a $1,000.00 check in July 2025 and a $2,000.00 check in April 2026) and $2,500.00 to **Texas Blue Action Democrats** (two $1,250.00 checks in May and June 2026) — an early and ongoing show of institutional alignment consistent with the ActBlue-heavy donor base above.

## Takeaways

1. **The candidate is self-funding more than his outside donors combined.** Between $32,800.00 in personal loans and $9,842.10 in in-kind contributions, Early has put roughly $42,642 of his own money and value into the campaign — well over 80% of the $51,641.00 raised in itemized outside contributions. For a challenger this early in a cycle, that's a meaningful signal about how much financial risk the candidate himself is carrying, and it's debt (the loans, at least) that would need to be repaid from future fundraising.

2. **Two donor relationships likely reflect family and household giving, not arm's-length support.** Wilson Early of Leander, TX gave $3,500.00 and lists no employer — plausibly family, given the shared surname and proximity to the candidate's Georgetown base. Separately, Elizabeth and Tracy Davis, both retired and sharing a Georgetown address, each gave the maximum $3,500.00 the same cycle — a household maxing out together rather than two independent donors. Family and close-network seed money at the top of a donor list is common for first-time challengers, but it underscores how early-stage this campaign's fundraising still is.

3. **Spending skews toward setting up infrastructure, not reaching voters.** Sixty-three percent of itemized spending ($66,122.72) falls under Administrative/Salary/Overhead, led by a single Waco firm's $17,661.58 for "website and general consulting" — more than double what the campaign spent on Advertising ($6,116.18). That's a normal pattern for a campaign's first several months, but it means the $104,402.20 spent so far has bought comparatively little direct voter contact.

4. **No House Ethics Committee financial disclosure is on file for this candidate.** Unlike sitting House members, who file annual Financial Disclosure Statements and Periodic Transaction Reports with the Committee on Ethics, no `house-ethics/` directory exists at all for Early in this repository — and federal law requires any candidate who raises or spends more than $5,000, which this campaign has, to file one. That threshold for filing (typically 30 days after crossing $5,000, or by the annual May 15 deadline) means this could reflect a filing that's simply not yet due, a pending extension, an actual compliance gap, or — just as plausibly — a Step 1 collection gap rather than an absence on the House Clerk's own site. It's worth checking directly against the House Clerk's public disclosure portal rather than assuming any one explanation.

5. **The donor base and outbound contributions both point toward a Democratic campaign, without FEC data stating it outright.** The large majority of itemized individual contributions ran through ActBlue, the campaign has given a combined $5,500.00 to the Texas Democratic Party and Texas Blue Action Democrats, and its PAC contributions came from labor unions (the Machinists and IBEW). None of that is a formal party-affiliation field, but taken together it's a consistent, unambiguous pattern in a race where the sitting incumbent, John Carter, is a longtime Republican.
